1 Background

This document presents a comprehensive landscaping proposal for a plot of land within Manjushree Park,
Chobhar, Kirtipur Municipality, Kathmandu Valley. The proposal has been prepared in conjunction with
Worldlink Communications' Environment Day plantation initiative, aimed at:

     · Enhancing green cover and ecological sustainability.
     · Beautifying the public recreational space through strategic fruit-tree and ornamental selection.
     · Creating an interactive, edible landscape for park visitors and local wildlife.

Manjushree Park holds significant cultural and ecological importance. Established to restore the landscape
scarred by a now-defunct cement factory at Chobhar Gorge, the park today serves as a peaceful recreational
retreat for both residents and tourists. It is named after Manjushree, the Buddhist bodhisattva credited in
legend with draining the ancient Nagdaha lake to create the Kathmandu Valley by cutting the Chobhar
Gorge.

2.2 Key Physical Features

Observations drawn from satellite imagery and field reconnaissance:

     · The entire planting strip sits directly adjacent to Dakchhinkali Road, a high-traffic two-lane highway.
       This is a critical safety and engineering constraint for all species selection.

     · The plot is a linear narrow strip running parallel to the road, approximately 25­35 m long and
       8­12 m wide between the road edge and the downslope treeline.

     · Flagstone-paved entrance / gate area at the road-facing boundary.
     · Gently sloping open grassy surface (approx. 5­8% gradient) descending from the road toward the

       forested gorge below.
     · Existing shed / shelter structure at the far downhill end of the plot.
     · Perimeter brick wall on the eastern side; a secondary access track runs along the eastern boundary.
     · Dense mature tree cover on the downslope side (Chobhar gorge forest) providing a natural backdrop.
     · Overhead power and utility lines run along the road corridor -- a key height-clearance constraint.
     · A roundabout and park entrance feature visible at the north-western end of the site.
     · Backdrop of Chandragiri and Champadevi hills providing scenic visual context.

2.3 Climate & Soil Context

Kathmandu Valley falls within a humid subtropical to warm temperate climate zone. The area receives
approximately 1,400 mm of rainfall annually, concentrated between June and September (monsoon).
Winters are mild with occasional frost at this altitude.

Soils in the Chobhar area are typically sandy loam to clay loam, moderate in organic matter, and slightly
acidic to neutral (pH 5.5­7.0) -- well suited for a wide range of fruit and ornamental trees. A distinctive
feature of the Chobhar locality is the presence of limestone in the underlying geology and soil profile -- a
legacy of the limestone deposits that once supplied the former Himal Cement factory at Chobhar Gorge.
As a result, the soil is locally calcareous (lime-rich) and tends slightly toward the alkaline end of the pH
range in some pockets. This favours lime-tolerant species such as Guava, Pomegranate, Lapsi, and Citrus,
which have all been prioritised in the species selection. Soil may also have been historically affected by
the cement bunker; visual inspection suggests these effects are not currently significant, but a basic pH,
free-lime, and texture test prior to planting is recommended.

3 Methodology & Plant Selection

3.1 Site Assessment Approach

The following engineering and horticultural criteria were applied to evaluate species suitability:

· Altitude suitability (mid-hill: 1,000­2,000 m).
· Root system type (deep vs. shallow -- critical near structures and paved areas).
· Canopy spread vs. available spacing.
· Risk classification (road hazard, utility-line interference).
· Water requirement and drought tolerance.
· Ecological benefit: soil binding, pollinator support, bird habitat.
· Community benefit: edible fruit, shade, aesthetics.

3.2 Revised Two-Site Zoning Framework

has therefore been restructured into a dual-site plan:

4 Site 1: Manjushree Park (Bhitta and Cheu)

4.1 Constraints and Strategy

Site 1 lies inside Manjushree Park, just behind the brick boundary wall that runs alongside Dakchhinkali
Road. Because the park interior faces the road, species selection here is strictly governed by road-safety
standards. Trees must:

    · Remain under 4 m at maturity to preserve vehicle sight lines and stay below utility lines.
    · Have non-spreading, deep tap-root systems that will not lift the road surface or pavement.
    · Be non-fruit-dropping onto the carriageway (no large fruit that could create road hazards).
    · Maintain a minimum 2 m clearance from the road edge.

Columnar growth forms are strongly preferred. Existing Jacaranda trees along the road frontage are to be
left undisturbed; new planting may approach them but should not overlap their canopy footprint. Behind
the boundary wall, slender trees and mid-height shrubs are permitted, provided roadside visibility is not
affected.

4.3 Layout

Trees are planted in a single row at 2 to 3 m spacing along two sub-zones within the park:

Bhitta (near-road edge, inside the boundary wall): the row of planting that runs immediately inside the

       utility-clearance constraints.

Cheu (far edge, no wall): the opposite edge of the park, set back from the road and the wall. Slightly
       larger crowns are acceptable here, but mature height is still capped at 4 m to keep a consistent low
       canopy across the park.

5 Site 2 -- Community Forest (Children's Park & Fruit Plantation)

5.1 Concept

Site 2 occupies a cleared zone within the adjacent community forest that has been earmarked as a future
children's park. The space will serve a dual purpose:

   1. A safe, landscaped recreation area for children with a central pathway, seating, and educational
       signage.

   2. A demonstration fruit orchard arranged along the pathway so visitors -- particularly children --
       can observe, learn from, and enjoy the trees.

Children's park functionality takes precedence: planted species are deliberately limited to those whose fruit
will not cause harm if it falls (no heavy fruits over walkways).

5.3 Layout

Fruit trees are arranged in a loose single (or double) row within the cleared children's park area at 5­6 m
spacing. Species are alternated -- Lapsi -- Pear -- Peach -- Plum -- Lychee -- Guava -- to provide

children and park users.

Existing Forest Buffer. The surrounding community forest is preserved as-is. No clearing is permitted
beyond the agreed children's park boundary. The dense surrounding canopy acts as a windbreak, maintains
soil stability on the gorge slopes, and provides natural ecological connectivity.

6 Engineering Considerations

6.1 Root-System Management

All trees planted within 3 m of the flagstone paving or shelter foundation must have deep tap-root systems
(e.g. Guava, Lapsi, Sissoo) to prevent surface heaving. Shallow-rooted species such as Eucalyptus are
explicitly excluded from this proposal.

6.2 Utility-Line Clearance

Utility and power lines run along the Dakchhinkali Road corridor directly adjacent to Site 1. Therefore:
     · No tree in Site 1 (Bhitta or Cheu) shall exceed 4 m mature height.
     · For Site 2, trees must be planted at a minimum horizontal offset of 3 m from any overhead line and
       shall not exceed 8 m mature height where lines are present.
     · Tall species (Walnut, Alder, Sissoo) -- where used at all -- must be set back well within the
       community forest, away from the road and utility corridor.

6.3 Slope & Drainage

The central slope has a natural gradient of approximately 5­8%. No grading or terracing is required. Trees
planted on the downslope side should be staked securely for the first two growing seasons.

6.4 Right-of-Way & Road Expansion

Future road-widening on Dakchhinkali Road must be considered in the design. The planting line is kept a
minimum of 2 m clear of the existing road edge, so any moderate widening will not require removal of
mature trees.

7 Technical Methodology for Fruit Plantation

Fruit trees require greater initial care, precise spacing, and nutrient-rich soil than standard forest trees in
order to ensure survival and future fruiting.

7.1 Phase 1 -- Site Layout & Spacing

A square or staggered triangular grid is recommended for Site 2. Crowding fruit trees reduces sunlight and
yields and increases pest vulnerability.

     · Guava, Peach, Plum, Pear: 5 m × 5 m (~16 ft × 16 ft).
     · Lapsi, Lychee: at least 6 m × 6 m due to large mature canopy.

7.2 Phase 2 -- Pit Excavation & Soil Conditioning

   1. Pit size: dig larger pits than for forest stock -- 0.75 m × 0.75 m × 0.75 m.
   2. Layering & conditioning: mix excavated topsoil with organic compost / farmyard manure (FYM)

       and neem cake (Neem ko Khali) in a 1:1 ratio. Neem cake is critical to prevent termites and root-rot
       diseases.

   3. Add a handful of bone meal or phosphate to encourage rapid initial root development.

7.3 Phase 3 -- Plantation Execution (Environment Day)

   1. Graft union management: most quality fruit saplings (citrus, peach, plum) are grafted. Ensure the
       graft union (the bump on the lower stem) sits at least 2­3 inches above the final soil level. Burying
       the graft union causes the tree to rot or lose its fruiting characteristics.

   2. Basin preparation: create a wide, ring-like soil depression (tha¯pala¯) of radius 0.5 m around each
       tree to act as a water-harvesting basin.

   3. Mulching: immediately after watering, cover the basin with a 2-inch layer of dry grass or straw.
       Mulching retains soil moisture, suppresses weeds, and regulates soil temperature.

7.4 Phase 4 -- Long-Term Sustainability & Protection

     · Tree guards: sturdy, tall bamboo tree guards are mandatory. Without them, saplings will almost
       certainly be trampled or damaged within weeks.

     · Handover protocol: as a Worldlink CSR event, a 1-year maintenance protocol should be formalised
       with the Manjushree Park caretakers and CFUG. Fruit trees require regular watering through the first
       winter dry season to survive until the next monsoon.

8 Park Design Recommendations (Site 2 Surroundings)

8.1 Land Preparation

Level the uneven grassy field to create a clean base for pathways, seating, and plantations; retain some
natural contours where possible for character and drainage.

8.2 Green Wall & Boundary Treatment

Use climbing plants (e.g. ivy, bougainvillea, local creepers) along walls and fences to soften hard edges and
blend the park with the surrounding landscape.

8.3 Plantation Strategy

     · Avoid very large canopy trees that could block views or reduce usable space.
     · Opt for medium-sized ornamental trees (Jacaranda, Gulmohar, Amaltas) and flowering shrubs for

       seasonal colour.
     · Introduce butterfly-attracting plants (lantana, milkweed, marigold) in clusters.
     · Where space allows, plant additional fruit-bearing trees (guava, pomegranate, citrus) for ecological

       and community value.

8.4 Pathways & Circulation

Lay permeable stone pathways to reduce water runoff and maintain a natural look. Curved paths can guide
visitors toward seating areas, gazebos, or scenic viewpoints.

8.5 Seating & Relaxation

Construct shaded sitting areas (gazebos, pergolas, benches under small trees) strategically placed near
plantations for a cool, relaxing environment. A central lawn can support yoga, picnics, or small gatherings.

8.6 Wildlife-Friendly Features

Install birdhouses on trees or poles to attract local species, and create small ecological pockets with
flowering plants to encourage butterflies and pollinators.

8.7 Water & Sustainability

    · Where feasible, add a small pond or fountain to enhance tranquillity and support aquatic plants.
    · Integrate rainwater harvesting for irrigation.
    · Provide composting bins to recycle organic waste into fertiliser for the plantations.

8.8 Information & Awareness

Place plant information boards to educate visitors about species and their benefits. Display park rules at
entry points to ensure cleanliness, safety, and respect for nature.

9 Summary & Approvals

CFUG Approval. Both Site 1 (Manjushree Park roadside strip) and Site 2 (Community Forest children's
park area) have been confirmed by the CFUG President for the Worldlink Environment Day 2026 plantation.

Deliverables. On Environment Day, the implementation team will:

   1. Plant Site 1 with columnar species (Pomegranate, Citrus, Paiyu, Kapur) under the 4 m height cap.
   2. Plant Site 2 with the alternating six-species orchard sequence (Lapsi -- Pear -- Peach -- Plum --

       Lychee -- Guava) along the central pathway.
   3. Install bamboo guards and water basins around each sapling.
   4. Hand over a 1-year maintenance protocol to the CFUG and park caretakers.

Expected outcome. A safe, attractive, low-maintenance dual-site plantation that meets road-safety and
utility-clearance constraints, supports local biodiversity, and provides an educational, edible landscape for
the Chobhar community.

10 Future Scope

Beyond the Environment Day 2026 plantation, the Site 2 children's park area offers significant potential for
phased enhancement in subsequent CSR cycles. Future expansion is envisaged in the following directions:

    · Playground equipment installation. Subject to site applicability and CFUG approval, age-
       appropriate playground materials -- such as swings, slides, see-saws, climbing frames, balance

                                                              12
Manjushree Park Landscaping Proposal      Worldlink Environment Day 2026

       beams, and a small sandpit -- can be installed within the cleared children's park zone. Selection and
       placement must respect the existing tree positions, slope, drainage, and the 5­6 m orchard spacing
       already established, so that play equipment is integrated around the fruit-tree layout rather than
       displacing it.
     · Safety surfacing. Where equipment is installed, impact-absorbing surfacing (rubber tiles, engineered
       wood-chip, or compacted sand) should be specified beneath swings, slides, and climbing structures
       in accordance with standard playground safety norms.
     · Shaded seating and gazebos. Permanent benches, pergolas, and a small gazebo can be added near
       the orchard pathway for caretakers and visitors.
     · Educational signage and a nature trail. Species labels, a short interpretive trail through the orchard,
       and information boards on the Chobhar limestone geology and Manjushree legend can convert the
       site into a low-key learning space.
     · Rainwater harvesting and composting. A small harvesting tank fed from any future shelter roof,
       together with on-site composting bins, would close the irrigation and organic-waste loop for the
       orchard.
     · Lighting and security. Solar-powered low-level path lighting and a basic perimeter fence around
       the children's park zone would extend safe usable hours and protect saplings.

All future-scope items are conditional on a fresh site-applicability check (ground conditions, slope, accessi-
bility, and CFUG consent) at the time of implementation.
